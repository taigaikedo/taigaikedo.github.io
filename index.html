About

I am a PhD candidate in Japanese linguistics in the Department of East Asian Languages and Cultures at Stanford University. My research draws on linguistic anthropology, sociolinguistics, pragmatics, and applied linguistics to examine how communicative conduct is interpreted and evaluated, and how those evaluations affect identities, relationships, and possibilities for participation.

My dissertation-in-progress examines how notions of appropriateness and communicative competence are understood across media and educational settings in Japan. It considers how language use, social interaction, technologies, and institutions inform judgments about communication and shape possibilities for participation.

Before coming to Stanford, I earned an M.A. in Japanese Linguistics from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, a B.A. in Applied Linguistics with a minor in Literary Studies from Winona State University, and a B.A. in English Studies from Aichi University.

Research

These questions guide my work on communicative evaluation, media circulation, and language education.

01

Evaluation and interaction

Appropriateness and communicative competence

How do people decide what counts as effective, appropriate, or skilled communication? I examine how such judgments emerge in interaction and how they position people as speakers, learners, participants, and evaluators.

02

Mediation and circulation

Language and media

I study how linguistic forms and social meanings circulate across digital platforms and other media, and how movement across contexts can provoke competing interpretations and evaluations.

03

Education and participation

Language education

I examine how classroom and peer interactions organize valued forms of communication and shape students’ opportunities to participate.
